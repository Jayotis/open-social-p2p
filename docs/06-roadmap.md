# Development Roadmap

**Status:** Stage 1 in progress. Milestones M0 to M4, and M5's replies and vouchers, are done and are the engineering foundation. The next release is the Product Alpha ([ADR 0030](decisions/0030-product-alpha-before-testers.md)), built around Home, the Universe and You; see below.

## Stage 0 — Requirements and research (enough done to start Stage 1)

What Stage 1 needed was decided. The rest doesn't hold it up, and each item says where it stands.

- [ ] Agree on the project's scope, contributors' expectations and non-goals. Drafted for discussion in [doc 01](01-vision-and-principles.md) and [doc 07](07-contributing-and-open-decisions.md).
- [x] Decide whether to build on an ActivityPub backend such as Mastodon or Friendica. Decided: a native protocol for Stage 1, with an ActivityPub bridge in Stage 2 ([ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md)).
- [x] Review libp2p, IPFS, WebRTC and relay models. Decided: iroh and iroh-blobs ([ADR 0002](decisions/0002-rust-and-iroh.md)). PeerTube is still to evaluate for longer video.
- [ ] Produce threat model and privacy requirements. A starting point is in [doc 05](05-security-privacy-and-governance.md), and the decision records settle privacy case by case. An independent review is in Stage 5.
- [x] Choose identity, data ownership, audience and media-manifest models. Decided for Stage 1: data stays on the home node ([ADR 0001](decisions/0001-home-node-architecture.md)), public content only ([ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md)), signed objects whose attachments carry their hashes ([ADR 0004](decisions/0004-signed-objects-and-ids.md)), and identity keys ([ADR 0009](decisions/0009-identity-backup-and-recovery.md), [ADR 0010](decisions/0010-signing-keys-and-revocation.md)).
- [ ] Specify adaptive peer grading and bandwidth-fairness goals. Proposals: [doc 03](03-peer-distribution-and-scoring.md) and [doc 12](12-peer-reciprocity-and-recognition.md). Only measuring is built ([ADR 0011](decisions/0011-measuring-peers.md)).
- [ ] Define open, independently implementable feed ranking, chronological mode and "Why am I seeing this?" explanations. Proposal: [doc 08](08-open-feed-algorithm.md).
- [ ] Research distributed public trend signals, aggregation privacy, federation coverage and Sybil/spam resistance. Research design: [doc 09](09-distributed-trending-signals.md).
- [ ] Evaluate manipulation scenarios before using P2P demand as any recommendation input. [Doc 09](09-distributed-trending-signals.md#anti-manipulation-research-requirements) says what to test; not evaluated yet.
- [ ] Specify optional exploration modes for overlooked work and topic-based differing perspectives. Proposal: [doc 10](10-exploration-divergence-and-hunters.md).
- [ ] Research verifiable discovery trails and anti-gaming measures for early curators ('hunters'). Proposal: [doc 10](10-exploration-divergence-and-hunters.md).
- [ ] Define voluntary offline referral links and private-by-default local discovery history. Proposal: [doc 11](11-collective-discovery-history-and-referrals.md).
- [ ] Assess bounded, short-lived P2P discovery snapshot proposals before any private-data aggregation. Proposal: [doc 11](11-collective-discovery-history-and-referrals.md).
- [ ] Describe licensing, documentation and governance policies. The code is under [AGPL-3.0](../LICENSE), and [doc 05](05-security-privacy-and-governance.md#open-governance-questions) lists what's still open.

**Gate:** Architecture decision records reviewed; basic publish/reshare/retrieve flows specified. The flows are specified in ADRs [0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md) to [0005](decisions/0005-media-profile-v1.md) and were built in M1.

## Stage 1 — Isolated desktop/P2P proof of concept (in progress)

Stage 1 is split into milestones:

- **M0 — decisions and skeleton (done):** [decision records](decisions/) 0001–0007, Cargo workspace, CI.
- **M1 — node-to-node prototype (CLI) (done; tested on one machine and across machines, direct and relayed):** identities, signed posts, follow tickets, outbox sync, media transcoding, reshare with hosting, verified retrieval with the origin offline. See [Stage 1 manual testing](testing/stage1-manual.md).
- **M2 — Android client and always-on node (done):** pairing by QR code, feed, playback, posting through the home node, on Wi-Fi and mobile data; the node runs as a service that starts at boot and reports its health to the phone.
  - Done: device pairing and the client protocol ([ADR 0008](decisions/0008-device-pairing-and-client-protocol.md)), health reporting, `osp service install`, and `osp-client` (the phone's networking, tested against a real node). See [M2 testing](testing/m2-phone.md).
  - Built: the Flutter Android app ([`app/`](../app/)) on top of `osp-client`: pairing, feed, playback, posting, reshare, follow codes and node health.
  - Tested on two phones against a node running as a service: pairing by QR code and by pasting, feed, playback with seeking, posting a photo, the offline banner, unpairing a phone while it plays, a relayed path standing in for a phone away from home, and the node starting at boot so both phones reached it before anyone logged in ([results](testing/m2-phone.md#results-to-record)).
  - Not tested on mobile data itself: the test phones have no SIM, so the relayed path on home Wi-Fi stood in for it. (The iPhone app was later tested on mobile data in M3.)
- **M3 — limits, resilience and the follower swarm (done):** upload limits with "busy, try another host", local peer assessment (measured first; [doc 12](12-peer-reciprocity-and-recognition.md)), follower swarm basics and staggered prefetch ([doc 13](13-hosting-roles-and-follower-swarm.md)), quotas and eviction, resumed transfers, a simulated slow/flaky network harness, and the relay-only privacy mode ([ADR 0007](decisions/0007-transport-privacy-defaults.md)).
  - Done: limits on what other nodes and devices can make a node do: requests, followers, the addresses it keeps, object sizes, sync, and the disk space their media takes, with quotas and eviction ([ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md), [ADR 0004](decisions/0004-signed-objects-and-ids.md), [ADR 0008](decisions/0008-device-pairing-and-client-protocol.md)).
  - Done: relay-only mode, opt-in, which hides the node's address from other nodes and phones, and only safe relays taken from other people's addresses ([ADR 0007](decisions/0007-transport-privacy-defaults.md) phase 1, [testing](testing/relay-only.md)).
  - Done: limits on posting media: one transcode at a time with time limits, a cap on posts in progress, and disk space set aside for an upload before it is sent ([ADR 0005](decisions/0005-media-profile-v1.md), [ADR 0008](decisions/0008-device-pairing-and-client-protocol.md)).
  - Done: limits on serving media: transfers at once (fewer for strangers than for followers, fewer with a slow upload, with places of their own for small files such as thumbnails), "busy" when they're all taken so the requester tries another host, a prefetch that fails or is refused as busy tried again later, a node that stops sending given up on after 30 seconds, and an optional upload speed cap ([ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md)). With the follower swarm on, a busy requester is pointed at followers who have a copy (below). A node with no cap sizes its transfers at once to the upload it has measured: in the simulated run with nine hosts in ten at 1 Mbit/s, every follower then had the video within 23 minutes, against 35 and 55 without it ([network conditions](testing/network-conditions.md#places-to-suit-the-upload)).
  - Done: follower swarm basics ([doc 13](13-hosting-roles-and-follower-swarm.md) phase 1, accepted 2026-10-24), behind a switch that `osp init` asks about, recommending yes; it's off without a yes ([ADR 0012](decisions/0012-sharing-prefetched-copies.md)). Prefetched media is its own class, `prefetch`, served to anyone when the owner shares it (`[swarm] share_prefetched`). A node that shares spreads its prefetches out over up to 10 minutes, tells the author's node when it holds a copy, and fetches from the followers that node names before the author. The author's node names them only to its followers, and only once it has checked a piece of the copy picked at random; of the 100 it names for a file at most, half are the followers that have followed longest, so a crowd of new followers can't push them out, and half those named last. One simulated home run with this spread the video as fast as before the check, or faster, but four later runs of the same node code had 95% of the followers holding it at 792 to 875 s instead of 617, so whether half and half undoes the slowdown of a full list is open ([network conditions](testing/network-conditions.md#checking-that-a-follower-holds-its-copy)). From the 100, each reply names six, first the followers heard from in the last 5 minutes, those reached directly before those through a relay: with a third of the followers offline, latecomers no longer asked ones that had stopped, and half had the video within 21 s instead of 30 ([network conditions](testing/network-conditions.md#naming-followers-heard-from-lately-first)). In the [B8 test](testing/b8-creator-upload.md#results) with 300 followers, the creator sent 20 copies of a new video instead of 300, and every follower had it within 14 minutes. With 100 followers, or a fifth of them stopping partway, the creator stayed within its mark and followers still had the video within 13 minutes.
  - Done: resharers' swarms (doc 13 phase 2, first half). A resharer that hosts a post's media keeps track of which of its followers hold a copy, and names them to its other followers, so people who see a post through a reshare get it from each other. The creator's node is always tried last. In the repeated B8 strangers run, 200 nodes following only the resharer all had the video 12 minutes after the reshare, against 152 after an hour before, and the creator sent 32 copies in all instead of 160.
  - Done: downloads in parts (doc 13 phase 2, second half). A file of 2 MiB or more, fetched through the swarm or opened on demand, comes in parts from up to three followers with a copy at once; one that finishes takes over from a slower one, and the author's node is asked only for what they couldn't send. In the [B8 runs](testing/b8-creator-upload.md#results), a download from the swarm was three times as fast as from one node at a time, all 300 followers had the video at 11 minutes instead of 13, and the creator sent 18 copies instead of 20.
  - Done: a responsive feed: a new post whose video or large photo is still on its way to the node is held back until it's there (at most 20 minutes), then shown on top, so a video tapped in the feed is usually on the node already. One that isn't is fetched from followers with a copy before its author, as prefetches are ([ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md)).
  - Done: media streams to the phone. A video the node doesn't have yet starts playing once its first bytes reach the node, instead of after the whole download, and the rest follows as it arrives. Several requests from the player share one download ([ADR 0008](decisions/0008-device-pairing-and-client-protocol.md)).
  - Done: the phone's device key kept in its secure storage, encrypted with a key held in the Android Keystore ([ADR 0008](decisions/0008-device-pairing-and-client-protocol.md)).
  - Done: phone uploads that carry on where they stopped after a broken connection or a restart of the app or the node, and are published once. The Android app keeps sending in the background, with a notification, and offers to carry on with or discard a post that didn't finish ([ADR 0008](decisions/0008-device-pairing-and-client-protocol.md)).
  - Done: the iPhone app, built on Codemagic and installed as an ad hoc build: the same screens as on Android, and a post keeps sending with the screen locked, its progress in a Live Activity (iOS 26 and later). Tested on an iPhone 12 on Wi-Fi and mobile data ([results](testing/m2-phone.md#results-to-record)).
  - Built: trusted followers ([ADR 0013](decisions/0013-trusted-followers.md)). A follower asks (`osp trust ask`), with a short note, and the owner allows or declines it (`osp trust requests`, `allow`, `decline`); the asking node is never told the answer. The owner can also trust someone they follow without a request (`osp trust add`). Requests are limited to one per node, 10 new ones an hour and 50 waiting. Trust grants nothing on its own; its one use so far is hosting: each `WhoHosts` reply names up to 3 trusted followers heard from lately among its 6, and up to 25 of a file's 100 places are kept for them. In a simulated home run with the slowest third of 300 followers trusted, every B8 mark held, and the followers not trusted still sent 82% of the copies ([network conditions](testing/network-conditions.md#trusted-followers)). On the phone, a badge shows requests waiting, a screen answers them and takes trust back, and the people you follow can be trusted or asked; checked with a test node asking another and the request allowed on a Pixel.
  - Done: local peer measurement, the first phase of [doc 12](12-peer-reciprocity-and-recognition.md): each node keeps a private, fading record of how the nodes it deals with behave (reachable, answering, downloads by outcome, bytes each way) and shows it with `osp peers`, without changing behaviour yet ([ADR 0011](decisions/0011-measuring-peers.md)).
  - Done: a simulated network harness, `osp-sim`, running a creator and hundreds of followers as real nodes in one process, and B8 measured with it ([plan and results](testing/b8-creator-upload.md)): in the baseline, before the swarm, a creator with 300 followers sent 8 copies of a new video, and the other 292 followers didn't get it ahead of time. The harness can also stop some followers partway through, and add strangers who see the post through a reshare. It can also run the nodes over a simulated network of home connections, with upload and download speeds, delays, packet loss and connections dropping out, with real QUIC over it ([plan and results](testing/network-conditions.md)). Before, a follower whose download was slow or stalled gave up instead of trying again, and in 3 of 5 home runs some followers never got the video. With that fixed, every follower got the video in each of the 21 runs, and on home connections the swarm met B8's marks in all six. With connections dropping out, followers met their marks in every run, but in one of six the creator sent 30.6 copies, just over its 30. With packet loss up to 2%, every follower got the video within 24 minutes and the creator's upload stayed low, but 95% of followers took 13 to 20 minutes, within the 15-minute mark in one run of six. With nine hosts in ten uploading at 1 Mbit/s, every follower got it within 38 minutes.
  - Done: release builds of the app signed with a key kept off GitHub ([app README](../app/README.md#release-signing)), and a CI check that no secret or key file is ever committed.
- **M4 — finding people and content (done as the foundation; moving media off n0's relays waits until after the Product Alpha):** today people find each other only through follow codes, and a post reaches someone only through whom they follow and what those people reshare. M4 lets people find others and their public posts with no single search service holding the index, seeing every search and deciding the ranking, and without showing strangers their home internet address. A decision record answers [doc 07](07-contributing-and-open-decisions.md)'s open decision 5: how people and content are found, who provides that, and what it reveals. Privacy shapes the design, so the decision comes before any product code. It draws on the proposals in docs [08](08-open-feed-algorithm.md)–[11](11-collective-discovery-history-and-referrals.md) (open feed, trending signals, exploration and hunters, discovery history and referrals) and on Stage 0's discovery research items, and brings forward the directories and lookup that [ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md) left for Stage 2. Its first goal is a few invited people finding and following each other without swapping follow codes.
  - **Use what works now, replace it later.** Tor's onion services carry messages between nodes from the start, instead of a forwarding network of our own built before there are people to run it. Only the home node runs Tor, since phones talk only to their own node, and video never goes over Tor. Media from nodes that hide their address goes through n0's public relays for now. Identities, posts and directories don't depend on either, so both can be replaced once the network has users.
  - In order:
  - **Tests first, side by side (done 2026-10-31):** two nodes on different networks talking through iroh's experimental Tor transport (`iroh-tor-transport`), measuring how long a connection takes, round trips and recovery after Tor restarts, with a packet capture showing nothing goes direct; full-text search with SQLite's FTS5 (already in our build) on real posts; and the B8 run in `osp-sim` with Tor-like delays on messages, to find which of M3's timers need changing. A throwaway test isn't product code, and its answers shape the decision record. Tor was quick enough for messages; the swarm met its marks with Tor-like delays, but the 20 s connection timeout didn't ([results](testing/network-conditions.md#messages-over-tor)).
  - **The decision record (done: [ADR 0014](decisions/0014-finding-people-and-private-messages.md), accepted 2026-11-01).** It covers three ways of finding, from nearest to furthest: the node's own index of what it has already received, searched at home so nothing leaves the house; directories; and exact lookups by key or domain. It also sets what is indexed and who may connect to a node directly.
  - **The private control plane** ([ADR 0007](decisions/0007-transport-privacy-defaults.md) phases 2 and 3 together). Today a node connects directly unless its owner turns on relay-only mode, which only matters while just the people holding a follow code can reach it. Once strangers can find a node, any of them could connect and learn its address. The Tor transport derives a node's onion address from its ID, so a node gets three endpoints, each with a key of its own: a public one reachable only through Tor, whose ID goes in its profile, follow codes and directory listings and reveals no address; a relay-only one for media, which has no address to give away; and a direct one, whose IP addresses are never published, for media and the node's own phones. A signed binding ties each of the other two to the public one, so a peer can check whose they are. Media follows a rule each side sets for itself: a direct connection only when both nodes allow it (for anyone, people they follow, contacts they approved, or nobody), and a relay otherwise. A node gives its direct endpoint only to the peers it allows, and the endpoint itself refuses any other. Following someone, or knowing them a long time, never grants it on its own. A private connection that can't be made fails; it never falls back to a direct one.
    Built so far (2026-11-01): the node's own Tor, the three endpoints and their bindings, and messages, the swarm, trusted followers and peer records on them, off unless a node's owner turns them on ([how](testing/tor.md)).
  - **Search on the node itself,** alongside: the posts and profiles it already has, working offline, with deleted and revoked posts dropped. Plain results; the open feed algorithm's ranking ([doc 08](08-open-feed-algorithm.md)) comes later. Built (2026-11-01): `osp search` and a search screen in the app, with revoked posts dropped; a post can't be deleted yet, so there are no deleted posts to drop ([how it works](decisions/0014-finding-people-and-private-messages.md#the-nodes-own-index)).
  - **Directories and lookup.** A directory is an opt-in role any node can take on, like the preservation node in [doc 13](13-hosting-roles-and-follower-swarm.md), and being listed is opt-in too. The maintainer runs the first one, so the first users find someone; the protocol lets anyone run another, and a node asks more than one, merges the answers and orders them itself. Profiles and posts are signed by their authors, so a directory can leave things out but can't fake them. Lookup by key turns an identity into its node. Impersonation: a signature proves a post comes from the same key as before, not from the person it names. So a creator can name their website in their profile, the website names their identity key, and the node checks both and shows the domain as verified; whether it's the right website is for people to judge. Without a domain, a profile works as before, marked as not verified. If a domain later names another key, followers are warned rather than moved. The first directory comes before the invited alpha; more directories, lookup and domains come after it (changed 2026-11-01, as a few invited people need neither).
  - **Nodes meet first ([ADR 0015](decisions/0015-nodes-meet-first.md), accepted 2026-11-01).** Comes with the first directory. A node on Tor also has a machine name, tied to no person, so it can get to know other nodes in the background and learn what's out there before its owner looks. The directory is its first acquaintance and a map of machines. A node fetches the directory's listing and searches it at home, so no word anyone types leaves the node. Reputation starts as each node's private record of the machines it has met; shared statements and introductions between people come later, with their rules set in the record. Built so far (2026-11-01): the machine name itself, on any node with Tor on; a directory's lists; a node reading them in the background and searching at home; and following someone found, with a node with no follows finding and following a stranger over real Tor. Built (2026-11-06): machines meeting machines on their own, within limits, so that a node left alone gets to know others and keeps doing so with the directory gone, and a crowd of fake names can't push out the machines it knows ([results](testing/meeting.md)).
  - **Measuring, alongside:** the B8 swarm in `osp-sim` with some followers in relay-only mode, counting how much video then goes through relays. Hidden addresses, cheap video, and nobody carrying extra traffic can't all hold at once, so this measurement decides media's default. Run (2026-11-07), with a simulated relay at n0's limits: with up to half the followers behind it the swarm met its marks; with all of them, today's default, it took over twice as long and missed two, and the relay carried 19 GB for one 52 MB video to 300 followers ([results](testing/network-conditions.md#media-through-relays)).
  - **Deleting a post** ([ADR 0019](decisions/0019-taking-posts-and-media-down.md)'s step 1, accepted 2026-11-06): a signed `Delete` that followers and resharers' followers act on, with tombstones so an old copy can't come back, and an undo in the app before it's sent. Built (2026-11-06), and its gate met in the simulator ([results](testing/deleting.md)).
  - **Moving media off n0's relays, decided before the alpha and built after it** (accepted 2026-11-08, revising ADRs [0016](decisions/0016-machine-acquaintances.md), [0017](decisions/0017-one-hop-forwarding.md) and [0018](decisions/0018-earned-direct-connections.md)). Media's default stays `nobody`. Nodes that say yes carry encrypted video between pairs without a direct link, over two hops. Each end, with its owner's yes to earned links, picks the one next to it, so its address is seen only by that hop: a host only its direct peers, a viewer also machines that finished jobs for it. Newcomers carry in the middle, where they see neither end. The node that received the file credits each hop for the job, and that standing gets service and counts toward an earned direct link, never access to content. Nobody picks peers, relays or hops by hand. Newcomers get turns at being met, with a limit on crowds, and machines can ask those they've met for introductions, one step at a time; these two came first, without waiting for the hops (built 2026-11-08). Introductions are off by default, as in [their run](testing/meeting.md#introductions-results) they helped one operator's names more than newcomers. [ADR 0022](decisions/0022-lists-and-introductions-are-earned.md) (accepted 2026-11-08) replaces them: a machine's list and its introductions go only to machines that did work for it that it checked, and a newcomer that knows nobody starts by carrying. Built after carrying. ADR 0017's stage A measures the hops in `osp-sim` before the alpha, in a model of three months ([plan](testing/hops-model.md)), beside [ADR 0023](decisions/0023-followers-keep-working-together.md)'s proposal that followers fetch first from those they've worked with.
  - **An invited alpha (replaced 2026-11-14 by the Product Alpha):** a few people, on their own Linux machines, were to install the node with Tor, find each other through the directory, follow and watch. It was ready as the `v0.1.0-alpha.4` draft, with a clean install tested on a lab machine, but there were no testers, and the app gave them little reason to stay. Those checks are now part of the Product Alpha's P11.
  - Not in M4: asking the nodes you follow (of little use while people follow few others), keyword search over a DHT (spam and poisoned results are hard to stop there), the open feed algorithm's ranking, trending signals, and a forwarding network of our own.

- **M5 — replies and vouchers (done; the rest of M5 became the Product Alpha below):** M5 was planned as a list of social features. Its first two were built, and the rest were replaced on 2026-11-14 by the Product Alpha ([ADR 0030](decisions/0030-product-alpha-before-testers.md)).
  - **Replies and conversations** ([ADR 0028](decisions/0028-replies-let-in-by-the-creator.md), accepted): signed public replies and threads in the native protocol and app, with limits for strangers, deletion and owner controls. This is also the native interaction model Stage 2's ActivityPub bridge will later translate.
    - Built: the creator's side. A node takes `SubmitReply` and `DeleteReply`, checks each reply, publishes those from the owner's circle and sends anyone else's to their requests under each post's policy (`[replies] default_policy`), within the ADR's limits. The owner answers requests, hides replies, allows or blocks repliers and sets policies (`osp replies`, `osp reply-requests`, `osp reply-publish`, `osp reply-decline`, `osp reply-hide`, `osp reply-policy`, `osp repliers`). Replies are kept apart from posts, and a node never stores one as a post, wherever it comes from.
    - Built: the replier's side. `osp reply` keeps a reply on the replier's node and sends it to the creator's, trying again for 30 days until it answers; `osp sent-replies` says where each stands, and deleting one sends the replier's `Delete` the same way. Threads of others' posts are read from the creator's media endpoint (`osp/replies/0`) through a viewing endpoint, so the creator isn't told who reads.
    - Built: the app. Each post has Replies: its thread, a box to reply in, and the owner's own replies with where each stands. On the owner's own posts it also sets who can reply, and hides replies or blocks their repliers. A badge counts replies waiting, and a screen publishes, allows, declines or blocks them.
    - Checked on real nodes and phones over Tor (2026-11-13): every step of the ADR's test met its expected result ([replies](testing/replies.md)). A reply sent while the creator's node was off arrived once, but the automatic tries missed while Tor couldn't reach the node, and `osp sync` sent it.
  - **Strangers' replies let in by vouchers** ([ADR 0029](decisions/0029-vouchers-within-six-steps.md), accepted): a stranger's reply is published without the creator looking when enough different people near the creator's circle vouched for them.
    - Built: step 1. A resharer's node tells the author's node of every reshare (`Reshared`), and the author's node keeps who vouched for its owner, within the ADR's limits; `osp show` counts a post's resharers. When a post has 10 resharers within 7 days, its first 3 get a signed `FoundEarly` credit, which their nodes keep.
    - Built: step 2. Any node answers "who vouched for your owner?" (`WhoVouched`, 10 questions an hour from one node): the newest vouch of each voucher, at most 32, each with the voucher's profile, leaving out those signed with revoked keys. Once a day a node gathers its owner's side: who vouched for them, then, asking those people's nodes, who vouched for those, and one step further, each person once at the nearest step, at most 256 people and 80 questions.
    - Built: step 3, on by default (`[replies] vouchers`) since step 4's round passed. With it on, a stranger's reply under `network` waits among the requests and its replier's node is asked to show its owner's side (`ShowVouches`, the one request allowed up to 256 KiB). The creator's node keeps only the vouches that verify, aren't signed by a key it knows was revoked, and chain back to the replier, and works out each person's steps itself. It places its owner's circle at 1 step and whoever those it follows reshared in the last 90 days at 2. If that isn't enough, it asks up to 16 of the circle's nodes (`PlaceWithin`, answered only for followers and the nodes of those followed, 60 an hour from one node, never passed on). It then applies the ADR's table, counting each meeting through the person in the circle it comes through, whichever node found it, and meeting points only as far as their chains to the replier are separate. At the mark the reply is published; below it, the request says what was found. The app shows how many people reshared one's own posts.
    - Done: step 4, `osp-sim`'s round of 300 nodes in 10 communities ([results](testing/vouchers.md#results)). The first round let every popular stranger in but missed the mark for work, which the maintainer raised to 150 ms on an SBC-sized node, and showed that whoever vouched for a careless resharer counted as the operator's meeting points; meeting points now count only along separate chains, with the table at 3, 4 and 6 points. On a fresh world, 28 of 30 popular strangers got in from 4 to 6 steps, and no throwaway reply did.

- **Product Alpha (next; [ADR 0030](decisions/0030-product-alpha-before-testers.md), proposed 2026-11-14):** M0 to M5's replies and vouchers are the engineering foundation. The next release is the first version someone who didn't help build OSP would want to install, explore, use, show someone and keep running. It replaces the invited mechanism alpha: the `v0.1.0-alpha.4` draft stays unpublished, and no release goes to testers before P11's gate. The app has three places, **Home · Universe · You**, with posting and Activity always at hand. The **Universe** draws the network as a zoomable map of people, content and machines. It's where people explore, and it shapes the node's new requests, the first run and the app's look.
  Protocol and network work interrupts this order only for a bug, a privacy or security problem, or something the node must do for the journey to work. ADRs 0017, 0018, 0022, 0023, 0026 and 0027 wait until after it.
  - **P0 — the reset (this entry).** The current app stays working as the reference while its screens move into the new shell. Nothing in the client protocol is removed while a screen still uses it. **Gate:** none; no tester release before the Product Alpha.
  - **P1 — the app shell** ([ADR 0031](decisions/0031-app-shell-home-universe-you.md)). Home (the feed), Universe and You; a Post button and an Activity bell everywhere; your node reached from your own star and from You; plain words, with Tor, endpoints, relays, logs and follow codes under Advanced. First run: this is you, your home computer is your node, then the Universe as the node finds people. An empty Home points to the Universe. The theme's tokens are set here. **Gate:** a new person can tell where Home, the Universe, posting, Activity and their profile are without knowing what Tor, iroh, an endpoint or a directory is; a test keeps those words off ordinary screens.
  - **P2 — profiles** ([ADR 0032](decisions/0032-profiles-people-want-to-follow.md)). A profile picture and up to 4 links in `Person`; the picture served to strangers through viewing identities; profiles edited on the phone (`SetProfile`); a profile page with posts, media, reshares, follow and "I know them". No follower counts for others, which nodes can't know. A verified domain shows once domains are built, after the alpha. **Gate:** finding a person, opening their profile, understanding who they are and following them, all in the app.
  - **P3 — what the Universe may show** ([ADR 0033](decisions/0033-what-the-universe-may-show.md)). One client request (`Universe`), built from one allowlist of what the node already knows and may show its owner: people, their steps from you and public reshare and vouch lines; machines in bands, never next to a person; content; totals. Never addresses, locations, viewing history, scores, or what was given on terms. Opening the Universe makes the node publish or ask nothing new. **Gate:** a privacy review of every field, and tests that snapshots hold nothing outside the list.
  - **P4 — the Universe in levels** ([ADR 0034](decisions/0034-a-universe-in-levels.md)). The node clusters people by public reshares into five levels (Universe, Galaxy, Cluster, Local, Close), keeps cluster IDs and positions stable, and sends only what's in view: at most 300 points and 600 lines, 128 KiB. Brightness is logarithmic and capped. A small world opens at Local. **Gate:** a synthetic world of 1,000,000 people explored within the limits; a world of 20 people that looks right; a stability test; smooth on the slowest test phone.
  - **P5 — exploring** ([ADR 0035](decisions/0035-exploring-the-universe.md)). Layers for People, Posts, Video, Shared and Network; tapping a group lists its people and posts, newest first; search stays on the node and lights up and flies to where its results are, with the plain list a tap away. Trending, hunters and other ranked layers wait for after the alpha. **Gate:** a newcomer with no follows finds interesting people and posts by browsing.
  - **P6 — the network at work** ([ADR 0036](decisions/0036-showing-what-the-network-does.md)). A short list of real events (directory reads, reshares, copies fetched and served, media kept available by a copy, machines met and faded) drawn on the map, and nothing made up. Your node's summary in plain words, with Advanced under it. **Gate:** a person who didn't build OSP can say in their own words what their node does.
  - **P7 — how are we connected?** ([ADR 0037](decisions/0037-how-are-we-connected.md)). ADR 0029's separate chains, shown on the map and in words, from the same code that admits replies; known lines, signed evidence and gaps kept apart, and nothing invented. **Gate:** in tests, the paths shown agree with the node's own calculation.
  - **P8 — likes and Activity** ([ADR 0038](decisions/0038-likes-and-activity.md)). A signed `Like` sent to the creator's node, counted on the post, with no part in standing, vouches, hosting or feed order; one Activity inbox on the node for replies, likes, reshares, followers and "says they know you". Android notifies; iPhones show Activity when opened. **Gate:** someone posts and sees the response in one place.
  - **P9 — the visual pass.** One visual system across post cards, profiles, avatars, media, type, spacing, empty, loading and offline states, the Universe and its transitions, light and dark, and accessibility (screen readers, text size, reduced motion). The Universe's look carries into the rest of the app. **Gate:** screenshots of Home, the Universe and a profile are recognisably OSP without the logo.
  - **P10 — sharing** ([ADR 0039](decisions/0039-sharing-your-universe.md)). A "Share my Universe" card from its own allowlist, previewed exactly, naming only people you tick; sharing a profile, a post or the install link. **Gate:** nothing shared discloses anything not already public or approved.
  - **P11 — hardening.** Fresh-node journeys, not developer nodes: install the node, pair, make a profile, enter the Universe, watch it find people, browse, open a profile, follow, see Home fill, post, like, reply, reshare, look at the network, and come back after a restart. Tested on a clean Linux install, x86_64 and arm64, Wi-Fi and mobile data, the creator on and off, Tor, node and app restarts, interrupted uploads and media, an empty and a growing network, a large synthetic Universe, the slowest test phone, and accessibility basics. The Universe's limits are measured here before they're fixed. **Gate:** a clean test user completes the journey without a terminal after installing.
  - **P12 — the Product Alpha.** Only now publish and recruit: the Linux installer, the phone apps, a refreshed README, a short install guide, a test guide, issue templates for bugs and usability, and a clear experimental and privacy warning. Recruiting starts with Show HN, pitched as a social network that runs from your own home node, with the network itself to explore as a zoomable universe, and a need for independent home nodes to see how it behaves outside the lab.

  **Gate:** P11's. The alpha has worked if independent people install it, understand the idea, find and follow others, return to Home, post or reply, explore the Universe unprompted, understand that their node takes part, keep it running after the test, and ask for improvements, not only report install failures. The strongest sign is someone sharing a screenshot or inviting someone without being asked.

  After the Product Alpha, unless testing changes the order: recommendations and trending (need real use and manipulation tests), hunters and divergent exploration (need a real content graph), communities and groups, private content, the ActivityPub bridge, a forwarding network of our own, dedicated video, hardware nodes, a desktop app, and large-scale moderation.
- **Before any public release:** identity backup and recovery ([ADR 0006](decisions/0006-desktop-first-always-on-node.md)). Built ([ADR 0009](decisions/0009-identity-backup-and-recovery.md)): recovery words, an optional encrypted backup file, and a `Moved` message so followers find a replacement node. Built ([ADR 0010](decisions/0010-signing-keys-and-revocation.md)): the identity key kept only as the recovery words, certifying a signing key on the node, so a copied signing key can be replaced or revoked, and followers drop what it signed.

Original Stage 1 checklist:

- [x] Pair two or more trusted test devices. (Two Android phones paired with one node.)
- [x] Share and verify a piece of public test media using content hashes.
- [x] Let a resharer optionally retain and serve the media.
- [x] Turn off the origin and retrieve from an authorized replica.
- [x] Measure transfer performance across fast, slow and unstable peers. ([Network conditions](testing/network-conditions.md), [B8](testing/b8-creator-upload.md).)
- [x] Enforce storage and bandwidth limits. (M3.)
- [x] Test peer discovery and relay/NAT scenarios. ([Manual tests](testing/stage1-manual.md), [relay-only](testing/relay-only.md), [Tor](testing/tor.md). Finding people is M4.)

**Gate:** Reproducible test results, including failure cases and privacy observations. Results so far are in [testing](testing/), and M4 adds to them.

## Stage 2 — Federation through an ActivityPub bridge (future)

Stage 1 uses a native protocol ([ADR 0003](decisions/0003-native-social-protocol-v0.md)) because ActivityPub delivers to each account's public HTTPS inbox, which a home node doesn't have. Stage 2 adds a bridge: a gateway with a public HTTPS address that translates between the fediverse and home nodes.

- [ ] Decision record for the bridge: who runs gateways and who pays, how a home identity maps to a fediverse account and its keys, and what a gateway can see. Or adopt an emerging standard if one suits home nodes better.
- [x] Identity backup and recovery first ([ADR 0006](decisions/0006-desktop-first-always-on-node.md), [ADR 0009](decisions/0009-identity-backup-and-recovery.md)): a fediverse account needs a stable identity.
- [ ] Map the native replies, likes and reshares to ActivityPub so interactions cross the bridge both ways.
- [ ] Media for fediverse servers, which fetch over HTTPS: the gateway fetches from home nodes and replicas, with caching and cost limits.
- [ ] Moderation, blocking and reporting for content arriving from other servers.
- [ ] Show bridged followers and replies in the app.

**Gate:** A Mastodon account can follow a home node, see its posts and media, and reply, without its user installing anything.

## Stage 3 — polished mobile and desktop early alpha (future)

By this point the Product Alpha has made the phone app a product for people who didn't help design it (Home, the Universe, profiles, likes, Activity). Stage 3 carries that to what the alpha left out, and brings a desktop experience up beside mobile.

- [ ] Build guided recovery journeys, and install on more than Linux.
- [ ] Carry Home, the Universe, profiles, conversations and the node's view to every supported client.
- [ ] Add clear availability, privacy and hosting controls without exposing protocol terminology unnecessarily.
- [ ] Complete the desktop user experience and keep mobile-friendly playback and caching behaviour consistent.
- [ ] Accessibility, usability and security testing on supported platforms.

**Gate:** A nontechnical test user can complete the core journeys without maintainer help and can understand the important privacy/hosting choices they are making.

## Stage 4 — Communities, video and broader federation (future)

- [ ] Define and implement communities/group permissions and events.
- [ ] Test dedicated video service and adaptive stream integration.
- [ ] Validate private-content architecture before enabling private P2P.
- [ ] Develop reporting, appeals and operator tools.
- [ ] Load test popular media distribution and estimate relay costs.

**Gate:** Documented service sustainability and governance assumptions.

## Stage 5 — Public release preparation (future)

- [ ] Independent security review and fixes.
- [ ] Clear privacy and content policies.
- [ ] Compatibility and migration documentation.
- [ ] Deployment, accessibility and disaster-recovery procedures.
- [ ] Reproducible packaging and release process.

## Success measures to agree on

User onboarding completion; original-node bandwidth saved; source distribution fairness; transfer success rate; median and tail media start latency; bytes carried by optional relays; privacy leaks discovered in testing; moderation response time; and fediverse users' ability to interact without installing anything.
